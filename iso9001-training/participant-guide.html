Module 0: Orientation

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

  1. Explain what a quality management system is.
  2. State why Meridian Group holds ISO 9001 certification.
  3. Describe the structure of the standard at a high level.
  4. Name the seven quality management principles.
  5. Identify your own responsibilities for quality.

Why it matters

Customers expect every output to meet their needs, every time. A quality management system helps Meridian Group deliver that result by design instead of by luck. When the system works, there are fewer errors, fewer complaints, and less rework. Your daily work is part of that system.

Key concepts

Quality management system (QMS). A QMS is the set of processes, rules, records, and responsibilities that lets an organization deliver consistent, conforming output. "Conforming" means that the output meets its requirements. An "output" is the product or service delivered, such as a component, a meal, a treatment episode, a software release, or a report.

ISO 9001. ISO 9001 is an international standard that sets requirements for a QMS. It applies to any organization, of any size or sector. It does not set product or service specifications. Customers and Meridian Group define those.

Certification. An independent certification body audits Meridian Group and decides whether the system conforms to the standard. An "audit" is a planned, documented check of evidence against requirements. Customers often rely on the certificate as assurance that the system is sound.

Structure of the standard. Clauses 1 to 3 give background: scope, references, and terms. Clauses 4 to 10 contain the requirements that are audited. They follow a Plan, Do, Check, Act logic:

  • Plan: understand context, lead, and set objectives (Clauses 4 to 6).
  • Do: provide support and carry out operations (Clauses 7 and 8).
  • Check: evaluate performance (Clause 9).
  • Act: improve (Clause 10).

Seven quality management principles. These ideas underlie the standard: customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, process approach, improvement, evidence-based decision making, and relationship management. A "process approach" means managing work as linked activities that turn inputs into outputs.

Edition status. Meridian Group is certified against ISO 9001:2015. A newer edition, ISO 9001:2026, has been reported as published, with a transition period. This guide teaches the 2015 requirements. Each affected module has a 2026 Bridge box.

What this looks like on our floor

Every record, form, checklist, ticket, and status label is part of the QMS. Frontline staff create most of the evidence that an auditor reviews. Examples from different sectors:

  • A machine operator records a measurement on a check sheet.
  • A nurse signs a medication round record.
  • A software support analyst logs the cause of a fixed ticket.
  • A driver completes a delivery receipt.
  • A site crew member signs an inspection checklist.

Technical staff keep equipment, test tools, and methods suitable for use. Supervisors make sure that people are trained, that records are complete, and that problems are escalated. In every role, you are expected to know your tasks, follow the current instruction, record what you did, and report anything that does not look right.

Common mistakes

  • Treating quality as the job of the quality department only.
  • Believing the system exists only for audit day.
  • Assuming that the standard tells Meridian Group how to make its outputs.
  • Confusing a certificate with a guarantee that nothing will ever go wrong.

Quick check

  1. Which clauses of ISO 9001 contain the auditable requirements?
  2. Does ISO 9001 define product or service specifications?
  3. Who generates most of the records in a QMS?
  4. What does the Check step of Plan, Do, Check, Act refer to?

Clause reference

Clauses 1 to 3 (scope, normative references, terms and definitions); Clauses 4 to 10 (overview); Clause 0.2 (quality management principles); Clause 7.3 (awareness).

Module 1: Context and Scope

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

  1. Explain why Meridian Group considers its external and internal context.
  2. Identify interested parties and one need of each.
  3. Describe what the QMS scope states.
  4. Describe a process using inputs, outputs, owner, and measures.
  5. Draw a simple map of your own process.

Why it matters

Quality problems often start outside the workstation: a change in customer demand, a late supplier, an ageing machine, or a shortage of skilled staff. If Meridian Group watches these factors, it can prepare before they harm the output. Customers benefit because delivery stays reliable.

Key concepts

Context (Clause 4.1). Context means the internal and external issues that affect Meridian Group's ability to deliver conforming outputs. External issues include market demand, technology, laws, competition, and supply conditions. Internal issues include workforce skills, equipment age, systems, and culture. Amendment 1:2024 asks organizations to decide whether climate change is a relevant issue.

Interested parties (Clause 4.2). An interested party is a person or group that can affect, or be affected by, Meridian Group's decisions. Examples are customers, regulators, employees, suppliers, owners, and the local community. Each has needs and expectations. Meridian Group tracks those that are relevant to its ability to meet requirements.

Scope (Clause 4.3). The scope states which products, services, sites, and activities the QMS covers. If a requirement of the standard does not apply, the scope explains why. An auditor uses the scope to decide what to examine.

Process approach (Clause 4.4). A process is a set of linked activities that turns inputs into outputs. Each process has:

  • inputs and outputs;
  • an owner, who is the person accountable for the process;
  • the resources it needs;
  • measures that show whether it works;
  • risks that could stop it from working.

The QMS is made of many processes that connect. The output of one step is the input of the next.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff. You can name who supplies your inputs and who receives your outputs. For example, a line operator receives material from the previous step. A ward nurse receives a care plan from a physician. A support analyst receives a ticket from the service desk.

Technical staff. You know which internal and external issues affect your equipment, methods, or systems. For example, a software engineer notes that a change in a third-party service affects a release. A calibration technician notes that a new customer requirement needs a tighter measurement.

Supervisors. You can name the owner and the measure of each process in your area. You pass on changes in customer needs or supply conditions to your team.

Exercise: draw your process. On one page, list five to eight steps of your work. For each step, write the input, the output, the owner, and one measure. Mark what the next step needs from the previous one. Ask whether any step has no measure. Sector examples are in the industry packs.

Example process maps from industry packs (fictional organizations):

  • Meridian Works (Discrete manufacturing): Receiving inspection → Cutting and machining → Assembly → Finishing and test → Final inspection → Pack and ship
  • Meridian Digital (IT and software services): Requirements intake → Design → Build → Test → Release review → Deploy and support
  • Meridian Care (Healthcare and clinical services): Referral and intake → Assessment → Treatment or procedure → Documentation and results review → Discharge or handover check → Follow-up

Common mistakes

  • Viewing each step as separate from the others.
  • Leaving process ownership unclear, so no one acts when the process fails.
  • Having no measure for a step, so problems stay hidden.
  • Treating context as a one-time document instead of a regular review.
  • Describing the scope too broadly or too narrowly compared with what is actually done.

Quick check

  1. Name two interested parties and one need of each.
  2. What does a process owner do?
  3. Why does the scope matter at an audit?
  4. Give one internal issue and one external issue that could affect your work.

Clause reference

Clauses 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4.

Module 2: Leadership and Policy

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

  1. Describe who is accountable for the effectiveness of the QMS.
  2. State the quality policy of Meridian Group in your own words.
  3. Explain what customer focus means in your role.
  4. Identify your own responsibilities and authorities, including authority to stop work.

Why it matters

A quality system works only when leaders set the direction and people at every level act on it. If leaders give quality low priority, staff soon learn that shortcuts are acceptable. If leaders provide resources, listen to problems, and back the right decision, customers receive consistent outputs.

Key concepts

Leadership and commitment (Clause 5.1). Top management means the person or group that directs and controls Meridian Group at the highest level. It is accountable for how well the QMS works. In practice, top management:

  • makes sure the policy and objectives fit the direction of the business;
  • provides people, equipment, and time;
  • promotes the process approach and risk-based thinking, which means considering what could go wrong or help before acting;
  • keeps attention on customer requirements and on risks to conformity;
  • supports other managers in leading their own areas.

Customer focus (Clause 5.1.2). Leaders make sure that customer and legal requirements are understood and met. They also watch for risks that could harm a customer's result. Customer focus is not limited to the sales team. Anyone who handles a work item, which is the unit of work that moves through a process, affects the customer.

Quality policy (Clause 5.2). The quality policy is a short statement of intent from top management. It must suit the purpose of Meridian Group, provide a framework for setting quality objectives, include a commitment to meet applicable requirements, and include a commitment to improve. It must be communicated, understood, and available to relevant interested parties. You do not need to recite the words. You need to explain what the policy means for your work.

Roles, responsibilities, and authorities (Clause 5.3). Top management assigns and communicates who does what and who may decide what. It also assigns someone to report on QMS performance and someone to keep the system whole when changes occur. An "authority" is the right to make a decision or take an action.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff. You can explain the policy using an example from your own station. A nurse might say that it means checking a patient identifier every time. A warehouse worker might say that it means scanning every pallet before loading. You know who your supervisor is and whom to call when something is wrong.

Technical staff. You know which decisions are yours, such as whether to take an instrument out of use or to approve a test result. You know which decisions need approval from someone else.

Supervisors. You know the authority you hold, including the authority to place a work item on hold or to stop a process that is producing nonconforming output. A "hold" is a status that prevents a work item from moving on. It may be a physical tag, an electronic block, or a paused release. Nonconforming means not meeting a requirement. You also show your team that raising a problem is welcome. When a person reports an error, you thank them and act.

Common mistakes

  • Memorizing the policy wording without knowing what it means for your work.
  • Unclear authority to stop work, so people wait for permission while problems continue.
  • Believing that leadership duties belong only to senior managers.
  • Reporting good news only, so leaders see an inaccurate picture.
  • Letting schedule pressure override a quality requirement without a decision by someone with authority.

Quick check

  1. Who is accountable for the effectiveness of the QMS?
  2. State the quality policy in your own words.
  3. Do you have authority to stop a process that produces nonconforming output? If not, whom do you call?
  4. Give one way your role affects the customer.

Module 3: Planning, Risk, and Change

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

  1. Explain risk-based thinking in plain language.
  2. Give one risk at your workplace and one control for it.
  3. Relate a quality objective to your daily work.
  4. Describe the steps of a planned change.

Why it matters

Most serious quality failures were possible to foresee. A tool wore out, a supplier delivered late, a key person left, or a change was made without checking its effect. Planning in advance reduces surprises. It protects customers from defects and delays, and it protects staff from rushed corrections.

Key concepts

Risks and opportunities (Clause 6.1). A risk is the effect of uncertainty on a result. In this context, it usually means something that could prevent conforming output. An opportunity is something that could improve the result. Meridian Group identifies risks and opportunities linked to its context and interested parties. It plans actions that are proportionate to the possible impact and checks later whether those actions worked. ISO 9001 does not require a formal risk management method. A simple list with owners and actions can be enough if it is used.

Quality objectives (Clause 6.2). An objective is a result Meridian Group aims to achieve. Objectives must be measurable, consistent with the policy, relevant to conformity and customer satisfaction, monitored, communicated, and updated when needed. A plan for each objective states what will be done, who is responsible, what resources are needed, when it will be completed, and how results will be judged.

Planning of changes (Clause 6.3). When Meridian Group changes the QMS, it does so in a planned way. It considers the purpose of the change, possible consequences, whether the system stays intact, whether resources are available, and who is responsible.

Climate change. Amendment 1:2024 requires Meridian Group to decide whether climate change is a relevant issue for its context and interested parties.

What this looks like on our floor

Examples of risks, with a control for each.

  • A measuring tool drifts. Control: calibrate it on schedule and check it before use.
  • Input from an external provider varies. Control: inspect on receipt and review supplier results.
  • A trained person leaves. Control: train a second person and keep instructions current.
  • A system or machine fails at a bottleneck, which is the step that limits overall flow. Control: planned maintenance and a backup method.

Examples of objectives. First-time-right rate, on-time delivery, complaints per unit of volume, rework rate, and response time. Your supervisor will tell you the current objectives of Meridian Group. OWNER INPUT

By role.

  • Frontline staff: know which objective your station influences. For example, an assembler affects rework rate. A service agent affects response time.
  • Technical staff: report risks you notice, such as a worn fixture or an unstable test script, and suggest controls.
  • Supervisors: keep the risk list for your area current, review progress on objectives, and make sure that no change is made without assessment.

A planned change in five questions.

  1. What is changing, and why?
  2. What could go wrong, and who could be affected?
  3. Who approves the change, and who carries it out?
  4. How will staff be informed and trained?
  5. How will we check that the change worked, and how can we undo it if it did not?

A change may be a new work method, a new software version, a new supplier, a new layout, or a revised process parameter. Do not make verbal changes without approval and a record.

Common mistakes

  • Setting objectives that cannot be measured, such as "do better."
  • Treating risk as paperwork that is completed once and forgotten.
  • Changing a process setting or method without assessing the consequences.
  • Informing some staff of a change but not all those affected.
  • Not checking whether a change achieved its purpose.

Quick check

  1. Give one risk at your station and one control for it.
  2. Which objective does your station influence?
  3. What must happen before a process parameter is changed?
  4. Does ISO 9001 require a formal risk management method?
  5. Why must staff be told about a change before it begins?

Clauses 6.1, 6.2, 6.3.

Module 4: Support

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

  1. Explain the resources that support quality: people, infrastructure, environment, and measuring tools.
  2. Describe the actions required when a measuring tool is overdue or found out of tolerance.
  3. Explain competence, awareness, and communication.
  4. Apply document control rules: current revision, correct records, no backfilling.
  5. Explain why training evidence is kept.

Why it matters

Good intentions fail without the right resources. A skilled person with a wrong instruction, an uncalibrated tool, or an unreadable record can still produce a faulty output. Support requirements make sure that the means of doing the work are sound, so that customers receive results they can trust.

Key concepts

Resources (Clause 7.1). Meridian Group provides what the QMS needs.

  • People (7.1.2): enough qualified staff.
  • Infrastructure (7.1.3): equipment, buildings, utilities, software, transport, and communication technology.
  • Environment for the operation of processes (7.1.4): physical conditions such as temperature, cleanliness, noise, lighting, and safety, plus social and psychological factors such as workload and respectful communication. Examples include cleanrooms, food hygiene zones, clinical infection control, site safety, and ergonomic workstations.
  • Monitoring and measuring resources (7.1.5): anything used to check conformity, such as a gauge, scale, thermometer, test script, or clinical device. Where measurement traceability is required, the resource is calibrated or verified at set intervals against recognized standards, identified so its status is clear, and protected from adjustment or damage. If it is found out of tolerance, Meridian Group assesses earlier results and acts.
  • Organizational knowledge (7.1.6): the knowledge needed for processes is determined and kept, including lessons learned.

Competence (7.2). Competence is the ability to apply knowledge and skill. Meridian Group decides what is needed, ensures people have it through education, training, or experience, acts where gaps exist, checks that training worked, and keeps evidence.

Awareness (7.3). You know the policy, the objectives relevant to you, how you contribute, and what follows if requirements are not met.

Communication (7.4). Meridian Group decides what to communicate, when, to whom, how, and by whom, both inside and outside.

Documented information (7.5). This covers documents and records. It is identified, reviewed, approved, and controlled for distribution, access, use, storage, change, retention, and disposal. Documents of external origin that the QMS needs, such as customer drawings or regulations, are controlled too.

What this looks like on our floor

  • Use only the current revision at the point of use, which is where the work is done. It may be paper, a screen, or a system. Check the revision before you start.
  • Remove obsolete copies. Do not keep photocopies.
  • Record data at the time of the activity. Write legibly, with initials or user identification and the date.
  • Never backfill, which means entering data later from memory. Never pre-fill a log.
  • Do not use a measuring tool whose calibration or verification is overdue. Label it, set it aside, and report it.
  • Do not perform a task you are not qualified to do. Ask a trainer first.
  • Technical staff keep calibration records and decide what to do about earlier results after an out-of-tolerance finding.
  • Supervisors check that training records match the tasks people actually perform.

Common mistakes

  • Personal cheat sheets that differ from the controlled instruction.
  • Photocopies kept after a revision.
  • Pre-filled logs.
  • Verbal changes with no record.
  • Overdue calibration or verification.
  • Training given but not recorded, or recorded but not checked for effect.

Quick check

  1. How do you verify that a work instruction is current?
  2. What do you do when a measuring tool or test script is overdue for calibration or verification?
  3. Why is training evidence retained?
  4. Why is backfilling a record a problem?

Clauses 7.1.2, 7.1.3, 7.1.4, 7.1.5, 7.1.6, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5.

Module 5: Operation, Part A: Requirements, Design, and External Providers

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Note on the 2026 edition: Clause 8 changes are reported as minor (confidence: Unclear). Verify against the published standard and the certification body. See Module 11.

Learning objectives

After this module, you can:

  1. Describe how Meridian Group plans and controls the processes that create its outputs.
  2. Explain how customer requirements are collected, reviewed, and changed.
  3. Explain, at a high level, what design control means where it applies.
  4. Name examples of external providers and state how their work is controlled.

Why it matters

Quality problems often begin before any work starts. An order accepted without review, a design that skipped a check, or an unapproved material can all cause failures later. Customers trust Meridian Group to promise only what it can deliver and to control what it buys. This module covers those early decisions.

Key concepts

Operational planning and control (8.1). A process is a set of linked activities that turns inputs into an output. Meridian Group decides what each process must achieve, how to judge success (the acceptance criteria), what resources are needed, and which records to keep. Planned changes are controlled. When a change happens by accident, people review what followed and limit any harm.

Customer requirements (8.2). A requirement is a need or expectation that is stated, implied, or obligatory. Staff communicate with customers about information, enquiries, feedback, and complaints. The company determines what is required, including legal and regulatory rules, and anything it considers necessary. Before agreeing to supply, the company reviews the requirements and confirms it can meet them. Differences between the request and the offer are settled first. If a customer gives no written requirement, the company confirms it before work begins. Records of the review are kept. When a requirement changes, documents are updated and the people involved are told.

Design and development (8.3). Design and development means turning a need into a defined product, service, or process. Examples include a new component, a care pathway, a software feature, or a building plan. Where it applies, the company plans stages, reviews results, checks that outputs meet inputs, tests that the result suits its intended use, controls changes, and keeps records. Meridian Group states in its scope whether design applies.

External providers (8.4). An external provider is any outside party that supplies something the company uses. This includes material suppliers, subcontractors, software and cloud services, temporary staff, calibration or testing laboratories, and transport firms. The company decides how much control is needed based on the effect on its outputs. It evaluates and selects providers, tells them what is required, and checks what arrives.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff

  • Check incoming consumables, such as materials, supplies, or access credentials, against the order before use.
  • Pass any change request from a customer to the supervisor at once.

Technical staff

  • Confirm that a service provider, such as a calibration laboratory, is on the approved list before sending items.
  • Record design review results and keep them with the project file.

Supervisors

  • Review purchase orders, contracts, or specifications before accepting them.
  • Raise a corrective action request when a supplier delivers late, wrong, or without proof of conformity.
  • Read supplier change notices and decide what they mean for the work.

Examples across sectors: a kitchen checks an ingredient delivery temperature; a clinic confirms a laboratory is approved before sending samples; a software team reviews a vendor library update before adopting it; a construction site checks that delivered steel matches the order.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting an order without checking capacity, skills, or deadlines.
  • Using an unapproved consumable because it is nearby or cheaper.
  • Ignoring or losing a supplier's change notice.
  • Treating verbal customer changes as informal and leaving them unrecorded.
  • Assuming a trusted provider needs no checks.
  • Skipping design review steps when a deadline is close.

Quick check

  1. Who reviews a changed customer requirement before work continues?
  2. What is verified when an externally provided input is received?
  3. Give one example of an external provider that does not supply materials.
  4. Why does the company confirm a verbal requirement before starting work?

8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4

Module 6: Operation, Part B: Production, Traceability, and Release

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Note on the 2026 edition: Clause 8 changes are reported as minor (confidence: Unclear). Verify against the published standard and the certification body. See Module 11.

Learning objectives

After this module, you can:

  1. Carry out work under the controlled conditions set by Meridian Group.
  2. Identify work items and mark their status correctly.
  3. Protect customer property, including data.
  4. Describe how outputs are preserved and released.
  5. Explain why changes to a process must be reviewed first.

Why it matters

Most defects and complaints arise while work is being done or handed over. Clear identification lets the company find a problem quickly and limit its reach. Careful handling protects what customers entrust to us. A disciplined release step keeps unchecked outputs from reaching the customer.

Key concepts

Controlled conditions (8.5.1). The company defines how work is done and what a good result looks like. This usually includes written instructions, suitable monitoring and measuring, working equipment, a suitable environment, and competent people. Some results cannot be fully checked afterward, such as a weld, a sterilization cycle, or a data migration. These processes are validated, meaning proven in advance to deliver the result. The company also builds in ways to prevent human error, such as checklists, prompts, and mistake-proof fixtures.

Identification and traceability (8.5.2). A work item is the unit of work moving through a process, such as a batch, job, case, ticket, or shipment. Each one is identified. Its status shows whether it is in process, on hold, checked, or released. Traceability means the ability to follow a work item back to its inputs and forward to its destination. Where traceability is required, each item has a unique identity and the records are kept.

Customer property (8.5.3). This is anything owned by a customer and placed in our care. It may be physical, such as material or equipment, or intangible, such as data, specifications, designs, or personal information. We identify it, check it on receipt, protect it, and keep it separate from other work. If it is lost, damaged, or unsuitable, we tell the customer and keep a record.

Preservation (8.5.4). Outputs must stay conforming until delivered. This covers handling, contamination control, packaging, storage, transmission, and protection.

Post-delivery activities (8.5.5). These include warranty, support, feedback, and complaints, as they apply.

Control of changes (8.5.6). A change to people, methods, materials, equipment, or software is reviewed before use. Records show what changed, who approved it, and what was done.

Module 7: Nonconforming Outputs

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

After this module, you can:

  1. Recognize a nonconformity in your own work.
  2. Apply the four steps STOP, FLAG, HOLD, REPORT.
  3. Explain why every disposition needs a record and an authority.
  4. Describe what happens when a nonconforming output has already been delivered.

Why it matters

Every organization sometimes produces something that does not meet its requirements. The harm comes when that output is used, shipped, or served by mistake. Fast, calm action protects customers and the people who rely on them. Early reporting also keeps the cost of the problem small.

Key concepts

A nonconformity is a failure to meet a requirement. A nonconforming output is a product, service, or result that does not meet its requirements. Examples are a part outside tolerance, a meal at the wrong temperature, a report with a wrong figure, or a software build that fails a test.

Meridian Group must identify and control such outputs so that nobody uses or delivers them unintentionally. The response depends on the nature of the problem and its effect. Options include correction, separation, containment, return, suspension of supply, informing the customer, and concession, which is written permission to accept an output that does not meet requirements. After correction, the output is checked again to show it now conforms. If the output was already delivered, the company also acts on the effects, such as notifying the customer or recalling it.

Records describe the nonconformity, the actions taken, any concession, and the person who decided.

The sequence: STOP, FLAG, HOLD, REPORT

  1. STOP. Halt the process if the cause is still active. Do not pass the problem on to the next step.
  2. FLAG. Mark the work item and any affected material. Use a physical tag or an electronic status.
  3. HOLD. Place it in a separate location or system status so it cannot proceed. A hold is a status that prevents a work item from moving on.
  4. REPORT. Tell your supervisor and the quality team at once. Give the work item identity, what you saw, and when.

Do not rework, redo, or scrap anything until a documented decision exists. Someone with authority decides the disposition, which is the action taken on the held item.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff

  • Apply STOP, FLAG, HOLD, REPORT without waiting to be asked.
  • Never move held items back into normal flow.

Technical staff

  • Investigate the extent of the problem: which other work items share the cause.
  • Propose a disposition and record the evidence.

Supervisors

  • Confirm the hold is in place and decide or obtain the disposition.
  • Check whether delivered items are affected and inform the customer when needed.

Examples: a machinist tags an oversized part; a nurse stops a medication round after a labeling error and reports it; a developer blocks a release after a failed test; a driver quarantines a damaged pallet.

Common mistakes

  • Quiet rework with no record.
  • "Just this once" release of a doubtful item.
  • Leaving suspect material at the station.
  • Reporting late, after more work has been done.
  • Assuming a small defect does not need a record.
  • Changing a disposition without the proper authority.

  4. Why must a corrected output be checked again?

Module 9: Improvement and Corrective Action

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

After this module, you can:

  1. Distinguish a correction from a corrective action.
  2. Complete a 5 Whys analysis for a simple problem.
  3. Explain why "operator error" is not a finished root cause.
  4. Describe how to verify that an action was effective.

Why it matters

Fixing the same problem again and again wastes time and harms customers. Corrective action stops repeat problems by removing their cause. Improvement more broadly helps Meridian Group become more reliable and useful each year.

Key concepts

Correction. An action that deals with the nonconforming output itself, such as reworking a part, reissuing a report, or repeating a service.

Corrective action. An action that removes the root cause, meaning the underlying condition that allowed the problem. Its aim is to stop recurrence.

When a nonconformity occurs (10.2), the company reacts by controlling and correcting it and dealing with the consequences. It then decides whether action on the cause is needed by reviewing the problem, finding causes, and checking whether similar problems exist or could occur. Next it carries out the action, reviews whether it worked, updates risks and opportunities if needed, and changes the system if needed. Records are kept.

Continual improvement (10.3). The company keeps improving the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of its system. Ideas come from data analysis, evaluation, and management review.

Root-cause methods. The 5 Whys asks "why" repeatedly until the cause lies in a process or system. A cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram sorts possible causes into groups such as people, methods, materials, equipment, and environment. A Pareto analysis ranks problems by frequency to show which few causes produce most defects. A root cause is rarely "operator error" alone. Ask why the system allowed the error.

Worked example: 5 Whys

Problem: A batch of labeled items fails final check because labels show the wrong date.

  1. Why were the dates wrong? The printer was loaded with the previous day's template.
  2. Why was the old template used? The operator did not reload it at start-up.
  3. Why was reloading missed? The start-up instruction does not list it.
  4. Why does the instruction omit it? It was written before the template system was introduced and never reviewed.
  5. Why was it not reviewed? No step ties a process change to an instruction review.

Correction: Hold and relabel the failed batch. Corrective action: Add template loading to the instruction, and add an instruction review to the change process. Training supports the fix but is not the only action. Effectiveness check: Three months later, review label results and confirm no repeat.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff: report problems promptly and give honest facts for the analysis. Technical staff: lead or join root-cause work and propose lasting fixes. Supervisors: assign owners and due dates, check effectiveness, and share lessons.

Examples: a hospital reviews a repeated missed-handover issue; a software team fixes a build step that allowed a defect; a plant updates a cleaning interval.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at "operator error".
  • Using retraining as the only action.
  • Closing the action without checking effectiveness.
  • Fixing only the one work item and calling it done.

Quick check

  1. Define correction and corrective action.
  2. Complete a 5 Whys for this problem: a delivery went to the wrong address.
  3. How do you verify effectiveness?
  4. Why is "operator error" not enough as a root cause?

Module 10: Audits: What to Expect

This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.

Learning objectives

After this module, you can:

  1. Name the main types of audit.
  2. Describe the steps of an audit.
  3. Explain common finding categories.
  4. Apply the response guidelines when an auditor speaks with you.

Why it matters

Audits show customers and certification bodies that Meridian Group does what it says. They also help find weaknesses before a customer does. Frontline staff create most of the evidence an auditor reviews, so your calm, honest answers matter.

Key concepts

Types of audit. An internal audit is done by the company's own trained staff. A customer audit is done by a customer to check its supplier. A certification audit is done by an independent certification body to decide whether a certificate is issued. Surveillance audits are smaller visits between certification cycles. A recertification audit renews the certificate.

The process.

  1. Opening meeting: the auditor explains purpose, scope, and plan.
  2. Evidence gathering: the auditor interviews staff, observes work, and reviews records.
  3. Findings: the auditor compares evidence with the criteria.
  4. Closing meeting: findings are presented.
  5. Corrective action: the company responds to each finding.
  6. Follow-up: the auditor or the company checks that actions worked.

Finding terms. Many certification bodies use major nonconformity for a serious failure of the system, minor nonconformity for an isolated lapse, and observation or opportunity for improvement for a suggestion. Terms vary, so Meridian Group confirms them with its certification body.

Auditors sample. They cannot check everything, so they pick examples. Evidence carries more weight than statements. A record or a demonstrated task proves more than a claim.

Response guidelines

  • Answer the question that was asked.
  • Be truthful. Do not guess. If unsure, say, "I will find out," and tell your supervisor.
  • Show the record or the instruction rather than describing it from memory.
  • Do not volunteer unrelated information.
  • Do not argue. If you disagree, state your point calmly and let the supervisor follow up.
  • If the question concerns another area, refer the auditor to your supervisor.

What this looks like on our floor

Frontline staff

  • Know the quality policy and how your work supports it.
  • Know your objectives, where controlled documents are kept, and how to place a hold.
  • Be able to show your training and the calibration status of equipment you use.

Technical staff

  • Keep calibration, maintenance, and change records current and findable.
  • Explain methods clearly and show the evidence.

Supervisors

  • Escort auditors and clarify scope.
  • Track findings to closure and share results with the team.

Examples: an auditor asks a nurse how a medication hold is applied; asks a warehouse worker where the latest procedure is kept; asks a developer to show the approval of a recent change.

Common mistakes

  • Guessing instead of admitting uncertainty.
  • Offering extra stories that open new questions.
  • Hiding or tidying records during an audit.
  • Treating the audit as a test of the person rather than the system.
  • Arguing over a finding on the spot.
  • Leaving findings open after the audit ends.

Glossary

Definitions are written in plain language for this program. They are not the wording of the standard. Terms are listed alphabetically. Sector terms for the selected industry pack are added at setup. Meridian Group may substitute its own terms.

audit. A planned, documented examination that compares what is done with a set of criteria, to find out whether requirements are met.

auditor. A person who carries out an audit. Auditors must be objective and impartial about the area they examine.

calibration. Comparing a measuring instrument with a known, traceable reference to find and record how far it is from the true value, and adjusting it where permitted.

concession. Written permission, given by an authorized person and where needed the customer, to use or deliver an output that does not meet a stated requirement.

conformity. Meeting a requirement. The opposite is nonconformity.

containment. Immediate action that limits the spread of a problem, such as stopping work, isolating suspect items, and identifying what else may be affected.

continual improvement. Repeated, deliberate effort to raise performance.

control plan. A document that lists what is checked at each step of a process, how, how often, and what to do when a result is outside its limit.

correction. Action that fixes a nonconforming output or condition that has already occurred. It does not remove the cause.

corrective action. Action that removes the cause of a nonconformity so that it does not happen again.

customer property. Anything owned by a customer and entrusted to the company, such as material, equipment, data, records, specifications, samples, and personal information.

documented information. Information that the company must control and keep, in any format. It includes instructions and procedures (which are maintained) and records (which are retained).

effectiveness. The degree to which planned work achieves the intended result.

finding. A statement of what an audit found, based on evidence, compared with the criteria. A finding may show conformity or nonconformity.

hold. A status that stops a work item from moving on, applied by physical segregation and tagging or by an electronic block.

interested party. A person or group that affects, is affected by, or sees itself as affected by the company's decisions or work, such as customers, regulators, employees, suppliers, and owners.

internal audit. An audit carried out by or for the company on its own system, at planned intervals.

measuring and monitoring resource. Any instrument, tool, software, or method used to check conformity.

nonconformity. Failure to meet a requirement.

objective evidence. Facts that can be checked, such as records, measurements, observations, and statements that can be verified.

output. The product or service the company delivers.

point of use. The place where work is done, such as a workstation, ward, site, desk, vehicle, or screen.

process. A set of connected activities that turns inputs into outputs.

process owner. The person accountable for how a process performs and for keeping it under control.

quality objective. A measurable result the company aims to achieve in support of its quality policy.

quality policy. A statement by top management of the company's overall intentions and direction on quality.

record. A document that states results achieved or gives evidence of an activity performed. Records are kept, not revised.

release. Formal permission, given by an authorized person, for a work item to move to the next stage or to the customer.

risk. The effect of uncertainty on an intended result. In daily work, it is something that could prevent a planned result.

root cause. The underlying process or system condition that allowed a problem to occur. Removing it prevents recurrence.

scope. The boundary of the quality management system: the products, services, sites, and activities it covers.

traceability. The ability to follow the history, use, or location of a work item, forward and backward, through records and identification.

validation. Confirming, by objective evidence, that a process or output will meet its intended use or requirements, particularly where later checking cannot prove the result.

verification. Confirming, by objective evidence, that a specified requirement has been met.

work instruction. A controlled document that explains how to perform a task step by step.

work item. The unit of work that moves through a process, such as a lot, job, case, deliverable, ticket, shipment, or client file.
