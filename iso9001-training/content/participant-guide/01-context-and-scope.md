# Module 1: Context and Scope

*This material is an original training aid. It is not a substitute for the standard. Obtain ISO 9001 from ISO or a national standards body.*

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you can:

1. Explain why {{COMPANY}} considers its external and internal context.
2. Identify interested parties and one need of each.
3. Describe what the QMS scope states.
4. Describe a process using inputs, outputs, owner, and measures.
5. Draw a simple map of your own process.

## Why it matters

Quality problems often start outside the workstation: a change in customer demand, a late supplier, an ageing machine, or a shortage of skilled staff. If {{COMPANY}} watches these factors, it can prepare before they harm the output. Customers benefit because delivery stays reliable.

## Key concepts

**Context (Clause 4.1).** Context means the internal and external issues that affect {{COMPANY}}'s ability to deliver conforming outputs. External issues include market demand, technology, laws, competition, and supply conditions. Internal issues include workforce skills, equipment age, systems, and culture. Amendment 1:2024 asks organizations to decide whether climate change is a relevant issue.

**Interested parties (Clause 4.2).** An interested party is a person or group that can affect, or be affected by, {{COMPANY}}'s decisions. Examples are customers, regulators, employees, suppliers, owners, and the local community. Each has needs and expectations. {{COMPANY}} tracks those that are relevant to its ability to meet requirements.

**Scope (Clause 4.3).** The scope states which products, services, sites, and activities the QMS covers. If a requirement of the standard does not apply, the scope explains why. An auditor uses the scope to decide what to examine.

**Process approach (Clause 4.4).** A process is a set of linked activities that turns inputs into outputs. Each process has:

- inputs and outputs;
- an owner, who is the person accountable for the process;
- the resources it needs;
- measures that show whether it works;
- risks that could stop it from working.

The QMS is made of many processes that connect. The output of one step is the input of the next.

## What this looks like on our floor

**Frontline staff.** You can name who supplies your inputs and who receives your outputs. For example, a line operator receives material from the previous step. A ward nurse receives a care plan from a physician. A support analyst receives a ticket from the service desk.

**Technical staff.** You know which internal and external issues affect your equipment, methods, or systems. For example, a software engineer notes that a change in a third-party service affects a release. A calibration technician notes that a new customer requirement needs a tighter measurement.

**Supervisors.** You can name the owner and the measure of each process in your area. You pass on changes in customer needs or supply conditions to your team.

**Exercise: draw your process.** On one page, list five to eight steps of your work. For each step, write the input, the output, the owner, and one measure. Mark what the next step needs from the previous one. Ask whether any step has no measure. Sector examples are in the industry packs. {{SECTOR_EXAMPLES}}

## Common mistakes

- Viewing each step as separate from the others.
- Leaving process ownership unclear, so no one acts when the process fails.
- Having no measure for a step, so problems stay hidden.
- Treating context as a one-time document instead of a regular review.
- Describing the scope too broadly or too narrowly compared with what is actually done.

## Quick check

2. What does a process owner do?
3. Why does the scope matter at an audit?
4. Give one internal issue and one external issue that could affect your work.
