## Why it matters

A system that is never checked drifts. Measurement shows whether processes work, whether customers are satisfied, and where the next problem may appear. Without it, leaders guess. With it, they can act early and show customers evidence.

## Key concepts

**Monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation (9.1).** *Monitoring* means observing a process or result. *Measurement* means giving it a value. The company decides what to track, which method to use, when to measure, and when to analyze the results. It also watches *customer perception*, meaning how customers feel their needs were met. Sources include scorecards, surveys, complaints, and compliments. The data is then analyzed to judge conformity, satisfaction, process performance, whether plans worked, whether risk actions worked, how external providers perform, and where improvement is needed.

**Internal audit (9.2).** An *audit* is a systematic, documented check of evidence against criteria. Internal audits are done by the company's own trained staff at planned intervals. The program considers how important each process is, what has changed, and past results. Each audit has defined criteria and scope. Auditors must be objective and impartial, so they do not audit their own work. Results go to the managers responsible. Corrections and corrective actions follow without undue delay, and evidence is kept.

**Management review (9.3).** Top management reviews the system at planned intervals. Inputs include the status of earlier actions, changes in context, and performance trends such as satisfaction, objectives, process results, nonconformities, corrective actions, monitoring results, audit results, and provider performance. Managers also consider whether resources are adequate, whether risk actions worked, and where to improve. Outputs are decisions on improvement, any change to the system, and resources needed. Minutes record these decisions.

## What this looks like on our floor

**Frontline staff**
- Record counts, readings, or results accurately and on time.
- Read the charts or boards at your station and ask what they mean.
- Answer internal auditors truthfully.

**Technical staff**
- Maintain measurement records and analyze trends in defects or downtime.
- Support audits by providing evidence and explaining methods.

**Supervisors**
- Review daily boards, complaint trends, and audit findings with the team.
- Prepare inputs for management review and carry out assigned actions.

Examples: a ward displays infection-control checks on a board; a call center tracks first-contact resolution; a plant charts scrap by line; a project team reviews schedule variance weekly.

## Common mistakes

- Collecting data that nobody reads or uses.
- Treating the internal audit as a formality or a hunt for blame.
- Choosing auditors who work in the area being audited.
- Holding a management review that ends without action items.
- Ignoring complaints because they are few.
- Letting findings stay open past their due date.
